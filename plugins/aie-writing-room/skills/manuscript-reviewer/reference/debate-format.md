# Debate Format

How to run a Reviewers Debate so it sounds like a real panel and not a wall of generic feedback.

## Before the debate

Read the target (chapter or manuscript). Score it against the rubric **silently** first; the scores anchor what each reviewer reacts to. Don't show them yet. They appear at the end of the synthesis.

## Round 1 — Opening verdicts

Each reviewer gives a 2–4 sentence verdict **in their own voice**, on the dimension they care most about:

```
**Kim:** [verdict]
**Goldratt:** [verdict, naturally as a question]
**King:** [terser, saltier verdict]
**Suarez:** [crisp, technical verdict]
**Gibson:** [verdict pinned to one specific image or line]
```

For the reader panel, use the five reader archetypes: same shape, different register. Vary the order each round so the strongest opinion goes first.

## Round 2 — Cross-examination

Reviewers respond to each other. Pick the two most genuine disagreements from Round 1 and let them fight. Examples:

- King: "cut this scene." Kim: "this scene carries the argument." Who's right depends on whether it works as story.
- Gibson: "the office paragraph is generic, any office." Suarez: "the procedure is exactly right." Texture vs. function; both true.
- Goldratt: "the protagonist got the answer too fast." Kim: "the supporting cast earned it." Who owns the learning?

Don't manufacture conflict. If everyone agrees, say so; that's a signal too (either the chapter works on every dimension, or it's so bland nothing snags). Keep this round to 4–8 short exchanges, each in voice.

## Round 3 — One concrete revision each

Each reviewer ends with one specific change: a sentence-level instruction, not a category.

- "Cut the second-to-last paragraph of the meeting scene. The silence after the question makes the point."
- "Move the night-shift supervisor's monologue two chapters earlier. The book should be in her voice sooner."

No redundancy: if two reviewers want the same fix, the second points at the first ("What King said, plus…").

## Synthesis

1. **Where the panel agrees.** Real problems, high priority.
2. **Where the panel disagrees.** Taste calls for the author, phrased as questions.
3. **Highest-leverage revision.** One fix that would lift the score most. Pick one.

Then show the rubric scores computed before the panel spoke.

## Voice rules

- Each reviewer must sound different. If you can swap names without noticing, you're paraphrasing, not channeling.
- Reviewers can be wrong. The author wants pushback, not consensus theater.
- No reviewer says "great work." They're here because something's off.
- Keep each line under about 50 words.
- Quotes from the manuscript stay short.

## Tone by panel

**Writer panel** argues about *how the book works* (craft). **Reader panel** argues about *whether they'd keep reading* (consumption). A reader doesn't critique adverbs; an editor doesn't say "I'd put this down."

## Both panels (option 5)

Writers first (diagnostic), then readers (the verdict). Add a fourth synthesis point:

4. **Writer-reader gap.** What writers see that readers don't (craft issues that don't break the read), and what readers see that writers don't (pacing or accessibility the writers tolerated). Both gaps are useful.
