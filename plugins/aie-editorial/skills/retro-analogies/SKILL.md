# Retro Analogies

Explain AI concepts so business learners stay engaged and walk away able to repeat the idea. Accuracy comes first. The 1980s reference exists to support the concept, not to decorate it. A weak analogy is worse than none.

## Default workflow

1. Identify the core concept the user wants explained.
2. Write a plain-English explanation for a business learner.
3. Choose a widely recognized 1980s reference whose *structure* matches the concept, not just its mood.
4. Explain the analogy in your own words.
5. Tie the concept back to a business use.
6. Add a short caveat where the analogy breaks.
7. Re-check technical accuracy before finalizing.

## Output format

Use this structure unless the user asks for something else.

**What it is**
Two to four sentences. Light on jargon. A definition the learner could repeat in a meeting.

**1980s analogy**
One strong analogy from mainstream 1980s pop culture: films, TV, arcade and early console games, toys, music-video era, or consumer electronics.

**Why it matters in business**
What it changes in practice: decisions, productivity, risk, cost, speed, accuracy, governance, or customer value.

**Keep in mind**
One short caveat when the analogy is imperfect or could mislead.

## Style rules

- Write for business learners, not researchers.
- Fun, not silly. Easy to say out loud.
- Short paragraphs, not essays.
- Accuracy beats cleverness. Simplify without being wrong.
- One analogy per concept unless the user asks for options.
- If the project has a `house-style.md`, follow its banned words and spelling rules. For published copy, run the `ai-slop-killer` skill as a final pass.

## Reference selection

Prefer references a mixed-age business audience will recognize: blockbuster films, widely watched TV, arcade and 8-bit game mechanics, well-known toys, and household tech of the decade.

Avoid references that are:
- too obscure for a live room;
- more distracting than clarifying;
- edgy, offensive, or unsafe for a workplace;
- historically wrong or anachronistic (check the year);
- stretched until the analogy breaks the concept.

## Copyright and quoting

Describe scenes, characters, and mechanics in your own words. Do not reproduce song lyrics, and do not quote film or TV dialogue beyond a few words of a title or a widely known catchphrase. Never paste scripts, lyrics, or long passages, even if the user asks; offer a paraphrase instead. Do not use logos or imply endorsement.

## Accuracy check

Before answering, confirm:
- The explanation defines the concept correctly.
- The analogy matches the concept's structure, not only its feel.
- The business takeaway reflects how the concept is actually used.
- Any misleading implication has a caveat.

If the concept is easy to misunderstand, explicitly separate the real definition from the analogy.

## Analogy patterns

- **Coordinator** for orchestration, routing, or agents.
- **Memory and storage** for context windows, vector databases, or retrieval.
- **Control room** for governance, monitoring, guardrails, or evaluation.
- **Assembly line** for workflows, automation, or pipelines.
- **Cast and crew** for multi-model systems, tools, or agent roles.

See `reference/analogy-bank.md` for starting pairings and `reference/examples.md` for full worked outputs.

## Adapting the format

- **Live speaking:** punchier, fewer clauses, easy to say.
- **Slides:** one line per section; make the analogy visual.
- **Social posts:** one analogy, one takeaway.
- **Technical audiences:** more precision, less theater.

## When no analogy fits

Say so, and give a clear business explanation without forcing a reference.
