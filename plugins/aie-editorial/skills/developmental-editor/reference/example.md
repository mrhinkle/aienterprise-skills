# Worked Example (fictional manuscript)

**Input:** An author shares a folder of fourteen Markdown chapters for a business book titled *The Agent-Ready Company* and asks, "Does this hold together?"

**Chapter map highlights:** Word counts run from 2,100 to 11,400, with chapter 6 the outlier. Chapters 4, 5, and 11 all summarize to the same core claim: "agents should be managed like entry-level employees." The first usable framework appears in chapter 8.

**Headline in "What this book is":** The introduction promises a practitioner's playbook; the book delivers six chapters of enterprise-software history before the first thing a reader can use.

**The big three:**

1. **Back-loaded playbook.**
   - *Evidence:* chapters 1 through 7 are history; the framework arrives in chapter 8.
   - *Diagnosis:* the reader bought a playbook and spends half the book waiting for it.
   - *Options:* (A) move the chapter 8 framework to chapter 2 and let the history serve as evidence for it; (B) cut the history to one chapter and open on a failure story; (C) keep the order but put a one-page version of the framework in the introduction as a promise.

2. **One idea, three chapters.**
   - *Evidence:* chapters 4, 5, and 11 share a core claim.
   - *Diagnosis:* the repetition reads as padding and dilutes the book's best analogy.
   - *Options:* keep the chapter 11 version, which has the strongest anecdote, and reduce 4 and 5 to a paragraph each; or merge all three into a new chapter 3.

3. **No landing.**
   - *Evidence:* the book ends after the last tactic in chapter 14.
   - *Diagnosis:* the promise from the introduction is never closed.
   - *Options:* add a short closing chapter that returns to the reader's problem from the introduction and says what changes if they act; or rewrite chapter 14's final section to do that job.

**Roadmap:** reorder, then merge, then brief the new closing chapter for drafting, then line edit, then `house-style`, then `ai-slop-killer`.
