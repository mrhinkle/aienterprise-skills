# Extraction Guide

How to turn raw samples into each section of `voice-profile.md`. Collect evidence before you write a finding. A finding with no evidence gets cut.

## Evidence rules

- **Three sightings make a pattern.** One occurrence is a note in your scratch work, not a profile entry.
- **Tag the register.** Record where each piece of evidence came from: casual, business, storytelling, community/values, humor, event/operational. Voices shift by register, and the profile should show how.
- **Quote short.** A calibration line is one to three sentences. Longer excerpts belong in the person's own archive, not the profile.
- **Speech versus page.** Transcripts show fillers ("like," "I mean," "right?"). Decide with the person how much of that survives into writing. The usual answer: keep the rhythm, thin the fillers by half or more.

## Dimension by dimension

### 1. Context that shapes the voice
Look for: career stages they mention, eras they lived through, places they are from, what they have built or run. Write it as the backstory a ghostwriter needs, in a short paragraph. Leave out anything they have not said publicly.

### 2. Rhythm and structure
Measure a sample of 30 to 50 sentences:
- Average and range of sentence length.
- How often sentences restart or change direction mid-clause (dashes, "I mean," "or rather").
- The landing move: a short declarative after a long buildup? A question? A shrug?
Write three to five bullets with one example each.

### 3. Storytelling moves
Ask of every argument in the samples: how did they make the point?
- Personal anecdote, data, historical parallel, named person, analogy, hypothetical?
- Where does the idea sit: before the story, inside it, or after as a punchline?
- Do they name real people, or use roles ("a CFO I know")?
Rank the moves by frequency. The top one is the primary unit of argument.

### 4. Values and worldview
Look for positions they repeat across contexts and would defend under pressure. Phrase each as a rule the writer must not break ("Prefers depth over scale; never frames growth as the goal by itself"). Five to eight rules is typical.

### 5. Verbal tics and signature phrases
Count them. Record each with its context and an approximate rate (per 1,000 words, or "every few paragraphs"). Separate:
- **Connectors** — fillers and transitions ("so," "right?").
- **Signature phrases** — recurring metaphors or frameworks the person owns.
- **Reaction markers** — how they introduce their own take ("and I'm thinking...").

### 6. Humor register
Where does the humor come from: specificity, irony, understatement, exaggeration, self-deprecation? Who is usually the target? What lines does it stay behind? Note whether jokes are set up or arrive inside the story.

### 7. Reference pool
List the eras, media, sports, industries, and history they reach for. This keeps a writer from dropping a reference the person would never make.

### 8. Tone by format
Build a table: format, length, structure, what changes from conversation. Fill only formats with evidence; label the rest "inferred" and keep them short.

### 9. Anti-patterns and banned words
Sources, in priority order:
1. Explicit statements ("I hate the word X," "never open with 'excited to announce'").
2. Corrections they have made to drafts.
3. Absence: registers or phrasings that never appear across a large sample.
Label each entry with its source type.

### 10. Calibration lines
Pick 6 to 10 lines that a reader would identify as this person without a byline. Spread them across registers. Check each against the privacy pass before it goes in.

## Confidence scoring

For each dimension, report high / medium / low:
- **High** — 5+ sightings across 2+ registers.
- **Medium** — 3 to 4 sightings, or one register only.
- **Low** — inferred, or from edited copy.

Put the scores in the profile's header so a future writer knows where to lean and where to ask.
