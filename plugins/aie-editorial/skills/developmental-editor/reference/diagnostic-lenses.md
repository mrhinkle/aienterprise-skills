# Diagnostic Lenses

Work the lenses in order. Each is a question. A healthy manuscript gets a short answer.

## Both fiction and nonfiction

1. **The promise.** What does the opening promise in subject, tone, and the kind of experience the reader is signing up for? Does the book keep it? Most structural failures are broken promises: a thriller opening on a book that becomes a seminar; a practitioner's playbook that opens with forty pages of history.

2. **The spine.** State the throughline in one sentence. If you cannot, that is the note. If you can, but a third of the chapters do not serve it, that is the note. For nonfiction this is the thesis, and Scott Norton's test applies: a thesis is a sharp, arguable claim, not a topic. "AI agents in the enterprise" is a topic. "AI agents should be managed like entry-level employees, and companies that skip that step will fail" is a thesis.

3. **Chapter jobs.** Every chapter must do something for the whole that no other chapter does. Write each chapter's job in a phrase. For nonfiction, use Josh Bernoff's test: "After reading this chapter, you will be able to..." If you cannot finish the sentence, the chapter has no job. Two chapters with the same job get merged. A chapter with no job gets cut or rebuilt. A chapter that can be moved anywhere without loss is not load-bearing.

4. **Proportion.** Does the page count go where the importance goes? The chapter map makes this obvious. A climactic turn on three pages while a setup scene takes twenty is a proportion problem, not a prose problem.

5. **Entry and exit.** Does the book start where the story or argument starts, or does it clear its throat? Does the ending land the promise, or summarize? Most manuscripts start one chapter too early and end one chapter too late. For nonfiction, cap the "here is the problem" material at one chapter; the reader already believes the problem is real, which is why they picked up the book.

## Fiction lenses

Tiffany Yates Martin's triage sets priority: character (do we care who this happens to?), stakes (does the character care profoundly? if not, the reader won't), and plot (does the journey change the character as a consequence of events?). Everything else is set dressing until those three hold.

6. **Stakes and escalation.** What does the protagonist stand to lose, when does the reader learn it, and does it get worse? Flat middles are almost always a failure of escalation, not a shortage of incident.

7. **Want versus need, and the arc.** For each major character: what do they want, why can't they have it, and what do they believe on page one that they no longer believe at the end? A character who is the same person at the close is either the point or a problem. Decide which.

8. **Scene versus summary.** Are the important moments dramatized and the unimportant ones summarized? Writers with a teaching instinct push key turns into exposition. Flag every place a decision is reported instead of shown.

9. **The lecture problem.** Business fiction dies when a character stops being a person and becomes a whitepaper with a name. Roz Morris's test for any scene carrying exposition: what changes for the characters as a result? If nobody learns, shifts, or reacts, it is information delivery, not drama. Could the speech be cut and the reader still understand the idea from what the characters do? If yes, cut it.

10. **Business-fable checks.** The books that work in this genre share mechanics:
    - Does the mentor figure ask rather than tell?
    - Is the protagonist allowed to be wrong and pay for it?
    - Do experiments fail? A common critique of *The Phoenix Project* is that almost none do, which undercuts its own lesson about learning from failure.
    - Are lessons distributed across characters with opposed temperaments (the cynic, the idealist, the operator) so ideas arrive as argument, not monologue?
    - Does recognition precede prescription? The reader should think "this happens everywhere" before the fix arrives.
    - Does the personal subplot carry the theme, or is it bolted on?
    - Is the book modulated with humor and relief so it is enjoyable and not merely accurate?

11. **POV and information management.** Who knows what, when, and does the reader find out in the most effective order? Any mystery or reveal lives or dies on this.

12. **Subplot integration.** Does each subplot pay off in the main line, or run parallel and end on its own? Parallel subplots are the first cut when a book is long.

## Nonfiction lenses

6. **Reader and job-to-be-done.** Who is this reader, what do they walk in believing, and what can they do when they close the book that they couldn't before? "Understand AI better" is not a job. Push for the behavior change. Check the emotional promise too: how is the reader's work different after they act, and what gets worse if they don't?

7. **Argument architecture.** Map the claims. Does each chapter depend on the one before, or is the order arbitrary? If the chapters could be shuffled without loss, the book is a collection, not an argument. That is fine if labeled and fatal if not.

8. **Evidence and citation.** Every factual claim needs a source in the house citation format. Flag assertions without sources, statistics without years, and fast-moving-field claims (AI, markets, regulation) older than about eighteen months. Evidence must be interpreted, not just displayed; a quoted stat with no "so what" is decoration.

9. **Story-to-instruction ratio.** A story that does not earn its lesson is padding; a lesson without a story is a slide deck. Bernoff's chapter template is a useful yardstick: opening story that presents a problem, the idea, a framework, argument with proof points, the idea restated, then actionable advice. Chapters missing the first or last element are the usual offenders.

10. **Redundancy.** Books written over months repeat themselves. "As we discussed earlier" is the marker. Find the idea explained three times and keep the version with the best story.

11. **Actionability and placement.** Where does the reader get the thing they can use (the checklist, the prompt, the framework)? All in the last chapter means the book is back-loaded. Nowhere means it fails its own promise. Modern business books often run closer to 45,000 words than the 65,000 to 70,000 of a decade ago; if the manuscript is long, say which chapters are paying rent.
